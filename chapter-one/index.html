Chapter One

The Door

by Davide Guerra

When Daniel Belmont reached Clara Weston's apartment, the door was already open by a few inches. He stood there with one hand still in his coat pocket, studying the dark line between the wood and the frame.

From somewhere down the corridor came the distant machinery of the lift. On the floor above, somebody had left a tap running, and beyond an ill-fitting window the wind worried at something loose against the glass. It was a cold morning, the kind on which Solaria seemed to wake reluctantly, not yet convinced the day had truly begun. Daniel looked at the door again.

“Has it been like this the whole time?”

The uniformed officer beside him nodded. “Since we went in.”

Daniel lowered his eyes to the lock. No obvious damage. No scratches, no splintered wood. He took in the handle, then the floor. A narrow trail of dust had been broken just in front of the threshold, as though something had crossed there without leaving enough of itself behind to matter.

“Who called me?”

“The Inspector.”

“That much I know.”

The officer almost smiled. “He said he'd rather explain in person.”

Daniel inclined his head. “Then something interesting must have happened.”

The officer looked towards the apartment. “A woman is dead.”

Daniel turned to him. “That makes it serious. Not necessarily interesting.”

The officer gave a short, uneasy laugh and stepped aside.

Daniel entered without hurry. The apartment was tidier than he had expected. No open drawers, no overturned chairs, nothing scattered in panic. The sitting room seemed to be waiting for someone who might come back at any moment: a book on the arm of the sofa, a lamp still burning although morning had already filled the windows.

He crossed the room without appearing to examine it, though he registered everything. The position of the curtains. A coat hanging near the entrance. Two shoes placed neatly beneath a console. An empty glass. A small scratch along the edge of the table.

He paused at a photograph on a shelf, looked at it for less than two seconds, and moved on. “Where is she?”

“Kitchen.”

Daniel faced the corridor. “Has she been moved?”

The officer hesitated. “Who?”

“The victim.”

“No.”

“Good.” Daniel took a few steps, then stopped again. “And the body?”

“Forensics have completed their first examination.”

Daniel gave him a look. “That isn't what I asked.”

Understanding came a moment late. “No. She was left where we found her.”

“Then let's go.”

Clara Weston lay on the kitchen floor, a short distance from the table. The room was small, lit by a window overlooking the street and by the strip above the sink, which someone had left on. Daniel remained at the threshold. There was no need to approach her yet. The first thing he looked for was not the body, nor the apparent cause of death. He wanted to see how a person had lived through the last few minutes before everything stopped.

A cup on the table. A kettle. A napkin folded carefully. An open file. Her phone beside the cup.

Daniel watched the phone longer than anything else. “Was it switched on when you arrived?”

“Yes.”

“Who touched it?”

“No one.”

“You're certain?”

The officer nodded. “We waited for forensics.”

Daniel stepped into the kitchen. “How many calls?”

The officer checked his tablet. “One missed.”

“When?”

“This morning.”

Daniel indicated the phone with his chin. “Before that?”

“Nothing.”

“Nothing?”

“Nothing.”

Daniel was quiet for a moment. He looked at the cup, the open file, then the phone again. “Who found her?”

“A colleague. Clara didn't arrive for work.”

“What time was she expected?”

“Half past eight.”

“The colleague called?”

“Yes.”

“How many times?”

“Once.”

Daniel lowered his eyes. “And then she came here.”

“Yes.”

“Before this morning, did anyone try to reach Clara last night?”

The officer looked at him. “What?”

“Calls. Messages. Someone looking for her.”

He checked the tablet again, more carefully this time. Daniel moved closer to the table and examined the file without touching it. Printed sheets, handwritten notes, a pen lying across the first page. The name Calveris Group appeared on one document. A date on the next. A signature on the third. He did not read long enough to pretend he understood them.

“No one,” the officer said. “No calls.”

“Messages?”

“A few. Nothing that stood out.”

Daniel nodded slowly. “All right.”

The officer looked up. “You think that's strange?”

“I don't know.”

“Then why do you keep asking?”

With one gloved finger Daniel shifted the cup by a fraction. “Because something can be ordinary and still feel wrong.”

The officer said nothing.

Daniel bent closer to the cup. The tea was cold, naturally, but not in any way that could tell him when it had been poured. Trying would have been theatre, and he had no patience for theatre at a death scene.

He straightened and looked at the file. “Did Clara handle these cases?”

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

“Nearly eight years.”

Daniel nodded. “Did she like the work?”

The question seemed to surprise the officer. “I wouldn't know.”

“A pity.”

“Why?”

Daniel gestured towards the papers. “Spend eight years checking documents and sooner or later you develop a particular relationship with mistakes.”

The officer's mouth twitched. “Do you develop particular relationships with everything?”

Daniel read the handwritten line at the top of the first page. “Only with things that keep returning.”

The reply came from the corridor. “Depends who you ask.”

Daniel turned. The Inspector had entered the apartment with a folder under his arm. He had the worn face of a man who had taken a call too early and spent the morning repeating the same facts to different people.

“To me,” the Inspector said as he approached, “she was a forty-two-year-old woman who had worked for Calveris Group for almost eight years. To her sister, she was someone who never phoned often enough. To her colleagues, she was the person who noticed errors in documents before they became problems.”

He stopped beside the table and looked down at Clara. “And to someone, apparently, she mattered enough to die.”

Daniel studied him. “You've decided it's murder?”

“The medical examiner hasn't finished.”

“So no.”

“So not yet.”

Daniel gave a small nod. “Good.”

The Inspector handed him the folder. “There are a few things we need to untangle.”

Daniel took it, but before opening it he looked once more at Clara's phone. One missed call, that morning. Everything else blank.

“Start with yesterday,” he said.

The Inspector opened his own file. “Clara left Calveris at 19:02. The time comes from her access badge. So far, no one can confirm seeing her after that.”

“Was she alone?”

“Presumably.”

Daniel looked up. “Presumably is doing too much work in this report.”

The Inspector gave half a smile. “And you use strange too often.”

“It earns its keep.”

“Sometimes.”

Daniel turned the page. It held access times, internal correspondence, and the names of three employees who had spoken to Clara during her final day. One caught his attention at once.

“Marco Deller.”

“Yes.”

“Who is he?”

“A colleague.”

“What kind of relationship did he have with Clara?”

“Professional. Officially.”

Daniel lifted his eyes. “Officially?”

“They argued yesterday.”

“About what?”

“A case file.”

“The one on her kitchen table?”

“Probably.”

Daniel closed his eyes for a moment. “That word again.”

The Inspector shut his folder. “The file concerns an internal review from three years ago. Clara reported discrepancies.”

“What kind?”

“We're still working that out. Deller says she was mistaken.”

Daniel ran a finger along the edge of the page. “When did they argue?”

“Before six. They both remained in the building afterwards. Deller says he left at 19:10.”

Daniel looked at Clara's badge record. “She left at 19:02.”

“According to the badge.”

“And according to Deller?”

“He says she was still at her desk shortly before he left.”

Daniel held still. “Then one of those facts is wrong.”

“Or Deller remembers badly. Or Clara used her badge and went back inside.”

“Both possible.”

“There.” The Inspector tapped the folder. “Three explanations already.”

Daniel closed it. “And not one piece of proof.”

For a few seconds neither man spoke. Daniel looked through the kitchen and along the corridor. “The cameras?”

“We're checking the building.”

“All of it?”

“Every internal camera.”

“And the street?”

The Inspector frowned. “Why?”

Daniel glanced towards the window. “If Clara left at 19:02, someone outside may have seen her. If she didn't, someone may have seen who went in.”

“We'll acquire the footage.”

Daniel opened the folder again. “There's something else.”

“What?”

“The call.”

The Inspector watched him. “This morning's?”

“Last night's.”

“We've checked.”

“And?”

“An incoming call shortly after half past eight. Unidentified number.”

Daniel looked up. “Unidentified?”

“Withheld. Three minutes, twenty-six seconds.”

He went still. “That wasn't in the first report.”

“We recovered it a few minutes ago.”

Daniel looked again at the phone. “Clara answered?”

“Yes.”

“Any calls after it?”

“No incoming calls. None made.”

“So the person behind that number was the last person known to have spoken with her by phone.”

“Yes.”

“And we don't know who it was.”

“Not yet.”

Daniel lowered his eyes. “Good.”

The Inspector studied him. “Something bothers you.”

“Too many things here could be normal.”

“That sentence means nothing.”

“I know.”

“Then what are you saying?”

Daniel pointed towards the phone. “A woman receives a call. Then she opens the door to someone. Then she dies. The next morning, no one can say with certainty who saw her last.”

“That is the investigation.”

“Exactly.” Daniel closed the folder. “That's the part I care about.”

The Inspector glanced towards the meeting room. “Deller says he didn't speak to Clara after the argument.”

“Do you believe him?”

“Not yet.”

“Good.”

“Want to talk to him?”

Daniel nodded. “Where is he?”

“Here. He's been waiting twenty minutes.”

Daniel checked his watch. “Let him wait a little longer.”

“Why?”

“To see whether he comes looking for us.”

“And if he doesn't?”

Daniel tucked the folder beneath his arm. “We learn something either way.”

Marco Deller waited. He did not come.

After a few more minutes, the Inspector asked an officer to bring him into the meeting room. Daniel remained outside, watching through the glass.

Marco rested both hands on the table, fingers linked. He checked his phone, moved it slightly, then looked at his watch. Nervous, Daniel thought. Not nervous enough.

Marco looked up and their eyes met through the glass. He put the phone down at once. Daniel entered.

“Marco Deller.”

The man nodded. “Daniel Belmont.”

“Have we met?”

“I've heard about you.”

“I hope it was flattering.”

Marco smiled faintly, a smile that arrived too soon. Daniel sat opposite him. The Inspector stayed near the door.

Marco looked between them and drew a breath. “I didn't kill Clara.”

Daniel placed one hand on the table. “I didn't ask.”

For a moment Marco had no answer. Then he looked down. “I know.”

“Why say it?”

“Because I knew you'd come for me.”

“Because of yesterday's argument.”

“Yes.”

The file was old, Marco explained. Three years old. Clara believed something in it did not add up; he had checked the original documentation and disagreed. Daniel let him speak until the difference appeared on its own.

“All of the documentation?” he asked.

“Everything available to me.”

Daniel lowered his eyes to the pen beside Marco's phone. Cheap blue plastic, transparent cap.

“That's a different answer.”

Marco frowned. “What is?”

“Clara found something?”

“She thought she did.”

“And you told her to stop.”

“She had no proof.”

“Was that the only reason?”

Marco met his gaze. “Yes.”

Daniel let the silence stretch. “How many times did you speak yesterday?”

“Twice. We talked around five, then argued shortly after six.”

“For how long?”

“Ten minutes, perhaps. Afterwards she went back to her desk. I went to my office.”

Daniel wrote the time in his notebook. Marco followed the movement of the pen.

“What are you writing?”

“What you told me.”

“Why?”

“Because you told me.” Daniel looked up. “When did you leave?”

“Seven ten.”

“Was Clara still there?”

“Yes.”

“You saw her?”

Marco looked down for the briefest moment. “At her desk.”

“Doing what?”

“Working.”

“On that file?”

“I think so. She had papers in front of her.”

“Did you say goodbye?”

“No. We'd argued.”

Daniel closed the notebook. “And you went home.”

“Yes. My wife can confirm it.”

“All right.”

Marco seemed surprised. “That's all?”

“For now.”

“I thought you'd ask more questions.”

“I will when I need the answers.”

Marco rubbed the back of his neck. “I had nothing to do with Clara's death.”

“That may be true.” Daniel watched him. “It may also be that you're trying to convince me of the wrong thing.”

“I'm not trying to convince you.”

“Everyone is.”

“Not me.”

Daniel almost smiled. “That's the part that worries me.”

He stood. At the door, he turned back. “One last thing. Clara received a call last night.”

Marco froze, but only for an instant. “I didn't know that.”

“I didn't tell you when.”

The silence thickened. Marco looked at the table. “Maybe I heard her talking.”

“Where?”

“At the office.”

“When?”

“I don't remember.”

Daniel nodded slowly. “Now you do.”

Marco swallowed. “What does that mean?”

“It means you knew which call I meant.” Daniel opened the door. “Good afternoon, Marco.”

The Inspector was waiting in the corridor. “Well?”

Daniel walked towards the lifts. “He lied.”

“We knew that.”

“Now we know where to press. He reacted to the call before I gave him enough information to recognise it.”

“He may have heard her.”

“Of course. Or seen her. Or spoken to whoever called. The problem is that a man questioned about a colleague's death should ask which call we mean. Marco didn't. He already knew what I was looking for.”

The lift arrived. Its doors opened with a soft metallic sound, but Daniel did not move. He could still see Clara's kitchen: the cup on the table, the phone with almost nothing on it, the single call the evening before.

Then he saw the entrance again. The hairline interruption in the dust by the threshold. The door open by a few inches.

“Wait.”

The Inspector stepped into the lift, then turned. “What is it?”

Daniel looked at the closing doors and then back along the corridor. “The door.”

“What door?”

“Clara's. It was open when we arrived.”

“Yes.”

Daniel stepped back. The lift closed without them. “That's what doesn't fit.”

“What did you see?”

“I don't know yet.” His voice had fallen. “I want to see it again.”

“Now?”

“Now.”

The Inspector followed him towards the exit. “You think we left something there?”

Daniel slowed. “I think we looked at that door the wrong way.”

He kept walking, his mind already back in the apartment. At the threshold. At the broken dust. At the detail that had seemed too small to deserve attention.

“We need to go back immediately,” he said.

“You've found something?”

Daniel stopped at the entrance and looked outside, then back at the Inspector.

“Yes.”

“What?”

Daniel lowered his voice.

“The door.”
