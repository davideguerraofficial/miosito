Chapter One

The Door

by Davide Guerra

When Daniel Belmont reached Clara Weston's apartment, the door was already open by a few inches. He stood there for a moment with one hand still in his coat pocket, studying the dark line between the wood and the frame.

The distant machinery of the lift travelled along the corridor. On the floor above, somebody had left a tap running, and beyond an ill-fitting window the wind worried at something loose against the glass. It was a cold morning, the kind on which Solaria seemed to wake reluctantly, not yet convinced that the day had truly begun. Daniel looked at the door again. It was neither wide open nor almost closed. It had stopped in a precise position, leaving just enough space for a narrow blade of shadow.

“Has it been like this the whole time?” he asked. The uniformed officer beside him confirmed that no one had touched it since they entered. He spoke quietly, although there was no one left inside to disturb. Daniel lowered his eyes to the lock. He saw no scratches, splintered wood or pressure marks around the handle, only a thin break in the dust near the threshold, so slight that a hurried observer might have mistaken it for the edge of a footprint.

The Inspector had summoned him without an explanation. When Daniel asked why, the officer hesitated and looked through the doorway before answering. “A woman is dead.”

Daniel allowed the words to remain between them. A death made every detail important, he said, but not necessarily significant. The officer lowered his chin, then stepped aside.

Daniel entered without hurry. The apartment was tidier than he expected from a place so recently surrendered to the police. No drawers stood open, no chairs had been overturned, and nothing appeared to have fallen during a struggle. The sitting room had preserved the shape of an evening interrupted gently: a book on the arm of the sofa, a lamp still burning in the morning light, an empty glass near the window. Nothing demanded attention, which made every object seem to hold it.

He crossed the room slowly. He registered the curtains drawn to the same height, a coat hanging near the entrance, two shoes aligned beneath a console and a recent scratch on the edge of the table. He paused at a photograph on a shelf. Clara was smiling beside another woman, both of them looking towards someone outside the frame. Daniel studied it for only a few seconds. Not long enough to imagine what existed beyond the photograph.

The officer directed him to the kitchen at the end of the corridor. Daniel asked whether Clara had been moved, and the answer forced him to clarify that he did not mean whether forensics had finished. He wanted to know if the body remained where it had been found. The officer checked his notes before confirming that it did. Daniel nodded and continued.

Clara Weston lay on the kitchen floor, a short distance from the table. The room was small and too bright. Its window overlooked the street; above the sink, a strip of light left on overnight flattened the softer shadows. Daniel remained at the threshold.

There was no need to approach her yet. Before the body, he searched for the life that had preceded it, for the final ordinary gestures made by someone who did not know they would be her last.

On the table stood a cup, a kettle, a carefully folded napkin and an open file. Clara's phone rested beside the cup, parallel to the edge, its screen dark. It looked like the least important object in the room, and Daniel watched it longer than anything else. The officer explained that it had been switched on when they arrived, that no one had touched it and that they had waited for forensics. The preliminary check of the visible device log showed one missed call that morning. Nothing appeared before it; the full acquisition was not yet complete.

Daniel made him repeat the word. “Nothing?” The officer checked his tablet again, this time without his earlier certainty. Clara had been due at Calveris Group at half past eight. A colleague called once and, when Clara did not answer, came to the apartment. According to the data available to them, no one had tried to reach Clara during the previous evening.

While the officer checked the call and message logs, Daniel moved closer to the table. Inside the file were printed sheets, handwritten notes and a pen left across the first page. The name Calveris Group appeared several times. A date headed the second document; the third carried a signature pressed hard enough to score the paper below. Daniel did not read long enough to pretend he understood the contents, but he noticed the order. Someone had arranged those papers to work on them, not to display them.

The officer found a few messages and no call that seemed important. He asked whether Daniel thought the silence was strange. The cup was not perfectly centred on the pale ring beneath it; a narrow crescent of the mark remained visible beside the base. Daniel studied it without touching anything and said that he did not know yet.

The tea was cold, but it could not tell him when it had been poured. Daniel made no attempt to extract a time from it. He returned to the file and learned that Clara had handled such cases for nearly eight years. When he asked whether she enjoyed the work, the officer could not answer. “A pity,” Daniel murmured. Eight years spent checking documents could teach a person to recognise recurring errors.

A voice came from the corridor. “Depends who you ask.” The Inspector entered with a folder beneath his arm and the tired face of a man who had taken a call too early. As he approached, he reduced Clara to three versions that did not quite agree. To Calveris, she was a forty-two-year-old employee with almost eight years of service. To her sister, she was someone who never called often enough. To her colleagues, she found errors before they became problems. Then he looked at the body and added that, to someone, Clara had mattered enough to die.

Daniel did not follow the implication. The medical examiner had not established murder, and he did not want a premature conclusion to shape everything that followed. The Inspector knew that caution and did not argue. He offered Daniel the folder, saying that several points needed clarification. Daniel took it, but before opening it he looked again at the phone. One missed call that morning, an almost empty screen and no obvious trace of the previous evening. “Start with yesterday,” he said.

Clara had left Calveris at 19:02, at least according to her access badge. No one had confirmed seeing her outside the building, and the report placed the word presumably beside the statement that she had been alone. Daniel observed that presumably was doing too much work for one word. The Inspector almost smiled, but he did not defend the report. They both knew that uncertain language became dangerous when repetition made it sound like fact.

The next page listed the last employees known to have spoken to Clara. Marco Deller's name carried a note about an argument shortly before six. It concerned an internal review closed three years earlier, in which Clara had reported discrepancies no one could yet describe clearly. Marco insisted that she had been mistaken. He claimed to have left at 19:10 and to have seen her still at her desk, even though Clara's badge recorded her exit eight minutes earlier.

The Inspector offered the most immediate explanations. Marco could have remembered badly, Clara could have used her badge and gone back inside, or one of the records could be wrong. Daniel listened without choosing among them. Three possibilities were not progress if there was no evidence capable of excluding even one. He closed the folder and looked from the kitchen into the corridor, imagining the same route the night before, when the table had been only a table and the door could stand open without anyone searching it for meaning.

The internal camera footage was already being collected. Daniel asked for the street cameras as well. If Clara had left at 19:02, someone outside might have seen her. If she had not, the street might have recorded whoever entered the building. The Inspector noted the request, then hesitated. Daniel waited without helping him.

The records just supplied by the network operator showed a call shortly after half past eight the previous evening. The number was withheld and the conversation had lasted three minutes and twenty-six seconds. Clara had answered. She received no further calls and made none herself. The kitchen, the table and the body remained unchanged, but those three minutes added a presence that had not been there before.

“Why wasn't it in the first report?” he asked. The Inspector explained that the first check had read only the log available on the phone. The operator's records had arrived minutes ago.

Daniel looked at the dark screen and imagined Clara at the table, the cup still warm, a nameless voice at her ear. Three minutes and twenty-six seconds were enough to arrange a meeting, ask for help or persuade someone to open a door. They could also mean nothing. Until they knew who had called, every possibility carried the same weight.

The Inspector asked what was troubling him. Daniel replied that too many things in the apartment could be normal, then realised the sentence explained nothing. He tried again, using only what they knew. Clara received a call. Later someone, perhaps the same person, came to the apartment. By morning she was dead, and no one could say with certainty who had seen her last. Everything else was a row of empty spaces they had already begun filling with assumptions.

Marco claimed that he had not spoken to Clara after their argument. He was waiting in a meeting room at Calveris and had been there for twenty minutes. Daniel decided to leave him a little longer. He was not trying to intimidate him. He wanted to see what Marco did when time stopped feeling neutral: whether he checked his phone, demanded an explanation or came looking for them. Marco stayed where he was.

Through the glass wall, Daniel watched him sit at the table. Marco checked his watch once, then remained still. When their eyes met, he did not look away. Daniel waited another few seconds, then opened the door.

Marco knew his name and said that he had heard about him. Daniel sat across from him while the Inspector remained near the door. Marco gave them both a brief nod and declared that he had not killed Clara. Daniel placed one hand on the table. “I didn't ask.”

Marco explained that he knew they would come for him because of the argument. He described the file as an old matter, an internal review closed three years ago. Clara believed that some of the data did not add up; he had examined the original documentation and disagreed. Daniel let him speak until Marco said he had checked everything available to him. It was not the same as checking everything. Daniel wrote down the sentence without commenting.

Beside Marco's phone lay a cheap blue pen with a transparent cap. Daniel watched it as he asked what Clara believed she had found. Marco said that, in her view, there was an error; in his, there was no proof. When Daniel asked whether that was the only reason he told her to stop, Marco answered yes at once.

They reconstructed the rest of the day without turning the interview into a volley of questions. Marco said that he had spoken to Clara around five and argued with her shortly after six. The argument lasted about ten minutes. Afterwards she returned to her desk and he went back to his office. Daniel wrote down the times and allowed the scratch of his pen to fill the silence. Marco followed each line as though the notebook might record something he had not said.

At 19:10, Marco continued, he left Calveris. He insisted that Clara was still at her desk, working through a set of papers. He did not know whether they belonged to the file, and he had not said goodbye because of the argument. Then he went home; his wife could confirm it. Daniel closed the notebook without challenging him. He would verify the badge, the cameras and the alibi before returning to that account.

Marco mistook the silence for the end of the interview and repeated that he had nothing to do with Clara's death. Daniel said that might be true, but perhaps he was trying to convince them of the wrong thing. Marco grew irritated and denied trying to persuade anyone. Daniel stood, slipped the notebook into his pocket and reached the door.

Before leaving, he turned. “One last thing. Clara received a call last night.”

Marco looked at him. “The one at half past eight?” Daniel said nothing.

For a moment Marco appeared to hear his own words; then he tried to take them back. He said that he might have heard Clara talking on the phone at the office, though he could not remember when. But Daniel had not given him a time, and the information from the network records had not been made public.

Daniel did not accuse him. He merely pointed out that Marco had identified a specific call before receiving enough information to distinguish it from any other. Marco asked what that was supposed to mean. “That you remember now,” Daniel replied. Then he opened the door and left him with the blue pen and an explanation he would have to repeat.

In the corridor, the Inspector caught up with him and said they had already known Marco was lying. Daniel shook his head. Until then, they had known only that Marco was concealing something about the argument. Now they had a precise point at which to press. He might have heard Clara, seen who called her or discussed the call with someone else. None of that proved he had killed her. It proved only that he knew about the call and had chosen not to mention it.

They reached the lifts. Daniel pressed the button and returned in his mind to the kitchen: the cup, the open file, the phone and the body a short distance from the table. Something was missing. Not from the room, but from the way they had looked at it.

The lift doors opened. The Inspector stepped inside, but Daniel stayed in the corridor. He was no longer thinking about Marco. He saw the entrance to the apartment, the thin light between the wood and the frame, and the break in the dust before the threshold. They had treated the open door as a condition of the scene. No one had asked what action would be necessary to leave it in exactly that position.

“Wait.” Daniel stepped back and let the lift close. He said that he wanted to return to the apartment at once. When the Inspector asked why, he could not yet give a complete answer. He knew only that they had looked at the door from the wrong side. As they headed towards the exit, the rest of the building seemed to fade around the image: the undamaged lock, the broken dust, those few inches of space.

At the entrance to Calveris, the Inspector asked whether he had found something. Daniel stopped with one hand on the door. Outside, Solaria had finally begun its day, but in his mind there was still only Clara's apartment.

“Yes.”

“What?”

Daniel lowered his voice. “The door.”
